Abstract

Autonomous unmanned systems, like UAVs and ground-based robots, are becoming an important part of disaster response. They make operations faster, safer, and more efficient by reducing the risks to human responders. UAVs can quickly map disaster areas, find survivors with thermal imaging, and restore communication when networks are down. Ground-based robots can inspect dangerous areas, remove debris, and take hazard samples in places that are unsafe for humans. Using systems like LiDAR, GNSS, and photogrammetry, these machines give responders accurate and detailed information to act on. Real-world events, such as the Australian bushfires and Nepal earthquake, show how effective they can be, but they also have limits like battery life, environmental interference, and high costs.

Introduction

Disasters cause chaos, and in such moments, time is critical. The response time can be the difference between life and death. Human responders face physical dangers, flooded zones, and wildfire fronts, which can delay access and cost lives. Autonomous systems offer safer, faster alternatives. UAVs map affected areas from the sky, detect trapped survivors using thermal imaging, and provide crucial aerial perspectives. Ground-based robots inspect unsafe areas, clear debris, and collect hazard samples in contaminated zones. These capabilities enable more efficient, targeted responses while minimising human risk, making them invaluable tools for modern disaster management.

UAVs

UAVs can be especially useful in many ways. Some include: mapping, thermal detection, sensor fusion, and swarm operations (Lyu et al., 2023). Mapping can be used in many ways. LiDAR can be used to accurately map an area even if the area is covered by smoke or dust, beating the traditional camera and allowing you to have an accurate look into a disaster when it's most critical (Jiang et al., 2021). GNSS (Global Navigation Satellite System) can also be used. This gives you a precise location and ensures mapping data is accurately geo-referenced so responders know exactly where each feature is located. Photogrammetry is also used — the science of taking multiple 2D pictures to make a 3D model while also taking measurements (Dadrass Javan et al., 2024). This, combined with LiDAR and GNSS, is critical. You have a highly accurate 3D model of the area thanks to LiDAR, with the photogrammetry giving you a highly detailed texture and visuals, as well as GNSS giving you precise coordinates. The combination makes for a fast, accurate, and visually clear 3D map, which allows first responders to know what to expect and prepare for. This helps responders prepare more effectively, possibly saving lives. UAV-BS (base station) units are deployed when cell towers are destroyed. They provide service and Wi-Fi, and they are optimised to focus on the ideal altitude and path to maximise coverage against battery drain (Fotouhi et al., 2019). This provides a network for survivors to contact aid teams as well as providing service for any UAVs and robots that need connectivity.

Ground-Based Robotics

Ground-based robotics can be used for a multitude of things, some include: inspection, hazard sampling, mobility, and tele-autonomy. Ground-based robots can inspect dangerous areas without the risk that a human would face. They can operate in collapsed structures, move on unstable debris, and even operate inside damaged pipelines (Wijayathunga et al., 2023). The ability to inspect these areas for possible danger or humans in danger is crucial. It allows responders to access areas that would be extremely difficult to reach and dedicate resources to areas that are especially critical or dangerous, saving time and lives. Hazard sampling is also useful. The robot can be equipped with chemical sensors for toxic spill sites, removing the risk to human hazmat teams. The robot could also be equipped with sensors such as a Geiger counter for radioactive sites. Physical samples can also be collected for further testing (Klamt et al., 2019). The mobility of a ground-based robot is also important to look at in disasters. For example, CENTAURO — a four-legged design capable of climbing, crouching, moving over rubble, and manipulating objects in confined spaces. This robot is equipped with two arms capable of using basic tools and moving rubble. The mobility of this robot, allowing it to move in confined and dangerous spaces, is crucial to emergency responses and indispensable for response teams (Klamt et al., 2019). This machine also uses tele-autonomy, which is when the robot operates on its own but will alert a human operator when it detects uncertainty. This allows for reduced human workload while maintaining safety in high-risk situations. It allows human operators to dedicate their resources to many places rather than just operating a machine (Wijayathunga et al., 2023).

Case Studies of impact:

Australian Bushfires During the deadly Australian bushfires, UAVs equipped with thermal infrared cameras detected active fire fronts and hotspots which were unable to be seen due to the dense smoke. UAVs equipped with LiDAR and photogrammetry were used to map terrain changes, identify fire breaks, and identify obstacles and hazards for ground crews. This allowed responders to establish better evacuation routes as a result of improved intelligence, as well as helping wildlife rescue teams to find trapped or injured animals in remote areas (Munawar et al., 2021). Nepal Earthquake A magnitude 7.8 earthquake struck Nepal on 25 April 2015, killing nearly 9,000 people and damaging over 600,000 structures. The mountainous terrain and the roads blocked with debris slowed first responders. UAVs were deployed to capture high-resolution imagery of the affected areas. Using LiDAR and photogrammetry, they created geo-referenced maps showing collapsed structures, blocked transport routes, and landslide areas. This enabled first responders to identify the most damaged areas and allowed the aid teams to focus on the areas most in need of aid, saving lives and helping survivors (Chen et al., 2022).

Cons of Autonomous Unmanned Systems in Disaster Response:

Some of the cons for autonomous unmanned systems are that they can only run for a certain amount of time, as they have a battery life that will wear out. Another con is that, in heavy smoke, dust, fog, or rain, the sensors will not be fully effective. This reduced effectiveness could nullify its use (Jiang et al., 2021). In areas of the world that have GPS-denied environments, there can be great challenges for the machine to navigate. This nullifies the use of the machine in certain parts of the world. These systems are highly advanced and complicated, and they cost a lot to build and maintain — especially advanced systems and sensors (Klamt et al., 2019). There can be highly sensitive data transmitted on those machines, and the risk of a cyberattack has to be addressed. With humans, they are able to provide aid even if their communications are cut off. The same does not apply for a robot — they are dependent on their communication systems, and if those are cut off, they become ineffective (Fotouhi et al., 2019).

Conclusion:

In conclusion, UAVs and ground-based robots improve the response time in disasters, allowing for a speedy, safe, and efficient response — saving countless lives and allowing responders to perform their job with less risk to their own lives. Systems integrated in UAVs, like LiDAR, GNSS, and photogrammetry, allow for actionable 3D maps to be used by response teams, allowing for a more precise and efficient response (Dadrass Javan et al., 2024). Ground units allow for inspection of the area to be thorough and efficient while minimising risk. The real-world references show us how great the potential and how indispensable autonomous unmanned systems are. However, they do have limitations like battery life, environmental interference, and legal issues limiting the abilities of the robots.
